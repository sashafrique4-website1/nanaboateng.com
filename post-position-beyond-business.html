Entrepreneurs Need to Position More Than Their Businesses

Don't let your company become the box you're known in.

By Nana Boateng · October 2026 · 6 min read

Entrepreneurs get boxed in more easily than we admit.

You start a business and become good at it. People recognize you for it, and your name becomes attached to your product, service, or industry. Eventually, the business and the person behind it are seen as one and the same.

That can be powerful for the business. It can also limit the entrepreneur. If you make sashes, you’re the sash guy. If you run a restaurant, you’re the restaurant woman. If you build houses, you’re the construction guy. The label may be accurate, but it is incomplete. Behind every business is a whole person who may have the capacity to contribute to conversations, institutions, and problems far beyond the industry they operate in.

Entrepreneurs spend considerable time positioning their companies: defining customers, sharpening value propositions, choosing categories, deciding what the market should associate with the brand. We rarely give the same thought to how we position ourselves.

I run Sash Afrique, a business built around celebrating achievers. I’m proud of it. But somewhere along the way, I realized I had to be careful not to let the product I build become the full description of the person I’m becoming. There is a difference between what you do and everything you are capable of doing.

Why perception shapes opportunity

That difference matters because perception drives opportunity. People recommend you for what they believe you can handle. They invite you into rooms based on what they have seen you demonstrate. Partnerships, leadership roles, board seats, and speaking invitations all depend partly on the picture others have formed of you. If that picture contains only your business, the opportunities that reach you will tend to stay within its boundaries.

Entrepreneurs who widened the frame

Some entrepreneurs have deliberately pushed past those boundaries. In Ghana, Alex Dadey has built a career across fintech, logistics, agriculture, property development, and commerce. He founded the KGL Group, was appointed to the governing board of the Ghana Investment Promotion Centre in 2018, and later became its board chairman (Ghana News Agency, November 2021). He has also been recognized for his contribution to Ghana’s digitalization agenda (Ghana News Agency, March 2022). For him, entrepreneurship is one dimension of leadership, not its entire definition.

Richard Branson is the more visible global example. He started Virgin in 1970 as a mail-order record retailer and went on to build businesses in travel, telecommunications, health, banking, music, and leisure. In 2004 he established Virgin Unite, the Virgin Group’s nonprofit foundation, and now spends much of his time on it and on organizations it has incubated, including The Elders, The B Team, and Ocean Unite (Virgin).

What interests me is not the size of their businesses or the fame that followed. It is their willingness to keep expanding what an entrepreneur can be.

What the journey teaches that travels

Many founders assume that once they’ve built a company, everything else must revolve around it. Identity becomes “founder,” conversations become sales, networks become industry-specific, and ambition becomes the next revenue target.

There is nothing wrong with building a great company, and it demands enormous focus. But focus shouldn’t mean permanent confinement.

Entrepreneurship teaches lessons that travel. You learn to decide with incomplete information. You learn about people, markets, capital, risk, and execution. You learn to negotiate, to manage difficult situations, and to take responsibility when things go wrong. You learn to turn an idea into something others will pay for or believe in.

Those lessons are valuable well beyond the company that taught them. An entrepreneur can bring them into a boardroom, a classroom, or a policy discussion. They can mentor young people, support a social cause, invest in other founders, or move into academia, public service, or community leadership. The range of possibilities is wider than we often allow ourselves to imagine.

The catch: you have to be visible beyond the business

If you want people to see more in you, you have to give them something to see.

Many entrepreneurs spend years talking about their products, sales, and customers, then wonder why no one recognizes their broader capabilities. People cannot position you for opportunities they don’t know you can handle.

So if you understand growth, talk about growth. If you care about education, contribute to the conversation. If building your company gave you a perspective on leadership, share it. If a social problem matters to you, get involved. If you believe you can contribute in a boardroom, start preparing for boardroom conversations.

Over time, people begin to see the wider picture.

This is why personal positioning requires more than visibility. It requires intention. Ask yourself what you want your name to represent in five, ten, or twenty years. Which rooms do you want to be invited into? Which problems do you want to be trusted to help solve? Then start building the credibility, relationships, and body of work that make those possibilities believable.

Two questions worth asking

A business expresses your ability to create value. It doesn’t have to mark the limit of your contribution.

Entrepreneurs often ask, “What does my business say about me?” A more useful question may be, “What does my business fail to say about me?”

There may be a strategist behind the founder, a teacher behind the entrepreneur, a policymaker behind the business owner, a mentor behind the CEO, a community leader behind the person signing invoices every morning. Sometimes the world sees only what we keep showing it.

At some point in an entrepreneur’s journey, building the company stops being the only kind of growth worth thinking about. There is also the growth of the person behind it.

Build the business, the team, the systems, the value. But also build yourself beyond the business.

One day, the question may no longer be how successful your company became. It may be what you did with everything the journey taught you. That is where the real unboxing begins: the moment you stop being defined only by what you built.
