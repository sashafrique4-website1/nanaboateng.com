How to Turn Customer Experience Into Growth

What serving more than 2,000 achievers has taught me about trust, loyalty, and the business value of making customers feel genuinely cared for.

By Nana Boateng · October 2026 · 7 min read

Customer experience has become one of those phrases that appears in almost every business conversation. We talk about service, satisfaction, retention, loyalty, and referrals. Yet after spending the past three quarters serving more than 2,000 achievers through Sash Afrique, I’ve come to see customer experience in a more practical way.

Customer experience is what remains with a customer after an interaction with your business. Did they feel understood? Did you keep your promise? Did they feel that their business mattered? And perhaps most important, did the experience give them a reason to tell someone else about you?

That last question has changed how I think about the subject. A business can advertise aggressively and acquire hundreds of customers. But when customers start voluntarily telling friends, colleagues, and family about their experience, something more powerful is happening: the customer is doing part of the marketing for you. That is where customer experience becomes a growth strategy.

The customer is buying more than the product

At Sash Afrique, we make sashes for achievers. On the surface, it is a simple product. But the more customers we have served, the more I’ve realized that nobody is really buying a piece of fabric. They are buying a representation of an achievement.

Behind every graduation is a story. Behind every promotion is a difficult season. Behind it all may be a family that supported someone through school, and the sacrifices, late nights, failures, and prayers that came before a moment worth celebrating.

Understanding that has changed how we serve people. We ask questions. We try to understand the achievement behind the order and look for details that make the final product personal. Sometimes a customer simply wants the sash. Other times, they want us to understand what it means to them. That distinction matters, because customers remember how a business made them feel.

Five things businesses can do to turn experience into growth

1. Perceive: Understand what the customer really wants

The first responsibility of a business is to understand its customer. This sounds obvious, but it is easy to miss. We become so focused on what we sell that we forget what the customer is trying to accomplish.

A customer may order a sash, but what she really wants may be to honor her journey, celebrate her achievement, and create a memory that lasts. The same is true across industries. A restaurant customer may want more than food. A banking customer may want confidence. A professional services client may want reassurance that they made the right choice. The better you understand the outcome your customer is after, the better you can design the experience around it.

2. Personalize: Make the customer feel seen

Once you understand the customer, make that understanding visible. Personalization doesn’t require sophisticated technology or expensive systems. Sometimes it is remembering a name, asking the right question, acknowledging a milestone, or taking an extra moment to learn someone’s story.

Recently, a customer needed a sash for someone being honored at a programme on Monday. The request came when our normal timeline made the deadline extremely difficult. Our standard production takes about four working days, and we don’t ordinarily produce over the weekend. But we understood what the deadline meant to her, so we stretched ourselves. We rearranged our schedule and pushed production to have the sash ready when she needed it.

After receiving it, she wrote: “Thank you so much. I like it.” It was a short message, but it reminded me that the value of customer experience is often in the effort the customer never sees. She may never know what happened behind the scenes. She simply knows that we came through for her.

3. Perform: Keep your promise

Customer experience ultimately comes down to execution. You can have the best intentions, but if you repeatedly fail to deliver what you promised, the customer’s perception will catch up with you.

This is where many businesses struggle. We promise what we hope to deliver rather than what we know we can deliver consistently. We underestimate timelines. We fail to communicate when things go wrong, then wonder why customers are frustrated. For a growing business, reliability is a competitive advantage. If you tell a customer something will be ready on Friday, Friday matters. If something changes, communicate early. If you make a mistake, own it. And if you can’t fulfill a promise, don’t hide behind silence.

We have not been perfect at Sash Afrique. There have been moments when we disappointed customers, and it would be dishonest to suggest otherwise. Customer experience is not a story of perfection. It is a story of learning, correcting, and getting better.

4. Pursue: Remember that the relationship doesn’t end at payment

Many businesses treat payment as the end of the customer journey. In reality, it is often the beginning of the relationship. What happens after the customer pays? Does anyone check that the product arrived safely? Does anyone ask whether the customer is satisfied? Does anyone take responsibility when something goes wrong? These moments tell the customer, “We still care.”

One of our customers lives outside Ghana. She found us on social media and, like many people who buy from online businesses, had reservations. She didn’t know us personally and had every reason to be cautious. She worked through the process with our customer service team and eventually received her package. What meant most to me was that she wasn’t only happy with the product. She was reassured that genuine businesses operate online and can be trusted to deliver. That is bigger than one sale. We had earned her confidence.

5. Propel: Give customers a reason to tell others

This may be the most important part of the framework. A satisfied customer may buy from you again. A customer who trusts you may recommend you. An advocate goes further and voluntarily tells other people about you because the experience was meaningful enough to share.

Think about the businesses you personally recommend. You probably don’t recommend them because of their advertising. You recommend them because something happened: someone treated you exceptionally well, solved a problem, delivered when they promised, or made a difficult process easier. You remember the experience, and then you tell somebody.

For a small or midsize business, this matters especially because referrals shorten the distance between a potential customer and the trust required to buy. So the question every business leader should ask is not just “Are our customers satisfied?” It is: “Have we given our customers a reason to tell someone else about us?”

Customer experience is a growth discipline

Customer experience shouldn’t sit exclusively within the customer service department. It belongs in the culture of the business. The person answering the WhatsApp message shapes it, and so do the people designing the product, running production, making the delivery, and leading the company. Every interaction either strengthens or weakens the customer’s confidence.

There is also an important balance. Going the extra mile for every customer, without discipline, can exhaust a business, and no company builds sustainable growth by operating permanently in crisis mode. The goal is to identify the moments that matter most to the customer and deliberately design the business to handle them well. That means understanding the customer, personalizing where it counts, keeping promises, following through, and creating experiences people feel compelled to talk about.

As we mark Customer Service Week, this is a useful conversation for founders, owners, and managers. We should celebrate our customer service teams, but we should also look at what the customer experiences across the entire business. Growth doesn’t happen only when we acquire a new customer. Sometimes it happens when an existing customer picks up the phone and says, “I have used these guys before. You should try them.”

That is when customer experience starts paying dividends. And perhaps the simplest test is this: What are your customers saying about your business when you are not in the room? That answer may tell you more than any survey ever will.
